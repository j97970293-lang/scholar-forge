# 11 — Manuscript structure and section rhetoric

## IMRaD as an argument, not a template
Every section answers one question:
- **Introduction** — why should anyone care, what is missing, what exactly did you ask?
- **Methods** — what did you do, and could a reader repeat it?
- **Results** — what did you observe (numbers), without interpretation?
- **Discussion** — what does it mean, what else could explain it, what are the limits?

## Introduction (CARS pattern)
1. Establish the territory with citations to primary literature.
2. Establish a niche: a gap, tension, unresolved method problem or contradictory finding.
   Every "few studies have…" needs evidence and a date.
3. Occupy the niche: purpose, research question, approach, contribution, and (optionally)
   the main finding in one sentence.

Do not write a literature review in the introduction. Do not promise contributions the Results
do not deliver.

## Methods
Enough detail to reproduce: design, setting, participants/materials, inclusion/exclusion,
procedure, variables and their operational definitions, instruments with versions, analysis
plan, software, ethics. Put the pre-specified primary outcome first. Use past tense and
active voice where it improves clarity.

## Results
- Follow the order of the pre-specified analyses. One paragraph per analysis.
- Report effect sizes with uncertainty, exact n, units and denominators.
- Point to the figure/table: "… (Figure 2, Table 1)".
- No interpretation, no new citations, no speculation.
- Include negative results and sensitivity analyses.

## Discussion
1. Restate the principal finding in plain language (no numeric repetition).
2. Compare with prior work — agreements and disagreements, with explanations.
3. Offer alternative explanations and rival hypotheses.
4. State limitations concretely: what they threaten and how large the bias could be.
5. Implications for theory/practice/policy, clearly labelled as such.
6. A short conclusion with no new claims and no overclaiming.

## Abstract and title
- Structured or unstructured per venue, 150–300 words: context, objective, design, setting,
  N, main result with a number and uncertainty, conclusion.
- No citations, no abbreviations that aren't defined, no "novel" unless the venue allows it.
- Title: specific, searchable, includes the design or population when it matters; avoid
  questions and hype. Keep a plain-language version for the lay summary.

## Paragraph mechanics
- One claim per paragraph, first sentence carries it, remaining sentences provide evidence,
  mechanism, qualification.
- Old → new information flow; vary sentence length deliberately (see `12-writing-quality.md`).
- Use transitions that express logic (however, therefore, in contrast), not decoration.
- Define acronyms once; keep a consistent term for each concept.

## Citation placement
Attach a citation to the specific claim it supports, not to a whole paragraph. Prefer primary
sources. Never cite a paper you have not read; never cite a review as evidence for a specific
primary result without reading that result.

## Common structural defects
- Results that contain discussion; discussion that introduces new results.
- A "gap" that is just "no one studied exactly this combination".
- Methods written after the fact, describing the analysis that happened to be significant.
- Limitations paragraph that apologises without naming a specific threatened inference.
